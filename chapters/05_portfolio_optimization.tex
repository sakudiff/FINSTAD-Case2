\section{Portfolio Optimization}

Mean-variance optimization establishes the mathematical foundation for modern asset allocation by identifying the locus of portfolios that maximize expected return for a defined level of variance, or equivalently, minimize variance for a target expected return. This section formulates the optimization framework, derives the analytical and numerical solutions for the Global Minimum Variance (GMV) and Maximum Sharpe Ratio (Tangency) portfolios, and provides an extensive econometric interpretation of the optimal asset weightings, the Efficient Frontier, and the Capital Allocation Line.

\subsection{Mathematical Framework and Convex Optimization Formulation}

Let the asset universe consist of $n = 6$ constituents with annualized expected return vector $\boldsymbol{\mu} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and annualized symmetric positive semi-definite covariance matrix $\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$. Portfolio allocations are represented by the weight vector $\mathbf{w} = [w_1, w_2, \dots, w_n]^T \in \mathbb{R}^n$. All allocations are restricted to the unit simplex $\Delta^{n-1} = \{ \mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^n : \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{1} = 1, \; w_i \ge 0 \; \forall i \}$, enforcing full investment and strictly prohibiting short sales.

\subsubsection{Global Minimum Variance Portfolio Formulation}

The Global Minimum Variance portfolio represents the leftmost boundary point of the Markowitz bullet, minimizing total portfolio variance without regard to expected return. Formally, the primal quadratic program is defined as:

\begin{equation}
  \min_{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^n} \quad \frac{1}{2} \mathbf{w}^T \boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{w} \quad \text{subject to} \quad \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{1} = 1, \quad w_i \ge 0 \quad \forall i \in \{1, \dots, n\}
\end{equation}

In the unconstrained benchmark case that permits short sales, the Lagrangian function is given by $\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}, \lambda) = \frac{1}{2} \mathbf{w}^T \boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{w} - \lambda (\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{1} - 1)$. Setting first-order stationarity conditions $\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} \mathcal{L} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{w} - \lambda \mathbf{1} = \mathbf{0}$ yields the classic analytical solution:

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{w}_{\text{GMV, unconstrained}}^* = \frac{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} \mathbf{1}}{\mathbf{1}^T \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} \mathbf{1}}, \quad \sigma_{\text{GMV}}^2 = \frac{1}{\mathbf{1}^T \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} \mathbf{1}}
\end{equation}

Under the long-only constraint ($w_i \ge 0$), the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker first-order optimality conditions require:

\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{w}^* - \lambda \mathbf{1} - \boldsymbol{\nu}^* = \mathbf{0}, \quad \mathbf{w}^* \ge \mathbf{0}, \quad \mathbf{1}^T \mathbf{w}^* = 1, \quad \boldsymbol{\nu}^* \ge \mathbf{0}, \quad \nu_i^* w_i^* = 0 \quad \forall i
\end{equation}

where $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$ is the Lagrange multiplier associated with the budget constraint, and $\boldsymbol{\nu}^* \in \mathbb{R}_+^n$ is the vector of dual Lagrange multipliers enforcing the non-negativity bounds. When an asset allocation is constrained to zero ($w_i^* = 0$), its corresponding dual multiplier $\nu_i^* > 0$ represents the shadow cost, measuring the marginal variance penalty that would be incurred if a non-zero allocation were forced upon the portfolio.

\subsubsection{Maximum Sharpe Ratio Portfolio Formulation}

The Tangency portfolio identifies the unique coordinate along the Efficient Frontier that maximizes excess return per unit of total risk relative to the Philippine 91-Day Treasury Bill baseline yield ($R_f = 5.25\%$ per annum):

\begin{equation}
  \max_{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^n} \quad \frac{\mathbf{w}^T \boldsymbol{\mu} - R_f}{\sqrt{\mathbf{w}^T \boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{w}}} \quad \text{subject to} \quad \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{1} = 1, \quad w_i \ge 0 \quad \forall i
\end{equation}

Because the Sharpe ratio objective is non-linear and fractional, it can be mapped into an equivalent strictly convex quadratic program \parencite{sharpe1964}. Applying the variable transformation $\mathbf{y} = \kappa \mathbf{w}$ with scaling factor $\kappa = (\mathbf{w}^T \boldsymbol{\mu} - R_f)^{-1} > 0$, the optimization maps to:

\begin{equation}
  \min_{\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^n} \quad \frac{1}{2} \mathbf{y}^T \boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{y} \quad \text{subject to} \quad (\boldsymbol{\mu} - R_f \mathbf{1})^T \mathbf{y} = 1, \quad \mathbf{y} \ge \mathbf{0}
\end{equation}

Upon solving for the optimal vector $\mathbf{y}^*$ via quadratic programming, the normalized portfolio weights are recovered through the relation $\mathbf{w}_{\text{Tangency}}^* = \mathbf{y}^* / (\mathbf{1}^T \mathbf{y}^*)$.

The empirical weights in Table~\ref{tab:optimal_weights} demonstrate how mathematical optimization exploits cross-asset covariance structures to satisfy divergent investment objectives.

The Global Minimum Variance portfolio concentrates 96.48 percent of its capital in domestic Philippine equities, allocating 39.82 percent to PLDT Inc. (\texttt{TEL}), 31.85 percent to Aboitiz Equity Ventures (\texttt{AEV}), and 24.81 percent to Manila Electric Company (\texttt{MER}). This concentration stems directly from the low standalone volatilities of Philippine utility and telecom cash flows, where annualized standard deviations remain bounded at 31.59 percent for \texttt{TEL}, 32.38 percent for \texttt{MER}, and 40.16 percent for \texttt{AEV}. Under the Euler risk decomposition theorem, the marginal contribution to risk must be equalized across all active positions in the minimum-variance state. Consequently, the optimizer pairs these domestic assets to minimize diagonal variance terms while exploiting low inter-asset correlations ($r_{\text{TEL, MER}} = 0.1187$, $r_{\text{TEL, AEV}} = 0.0884$, $r_{\text{MER, AEV}} = 0.2959$).

A notable empirical finding is the 3.52 percent allocation assigned to NVIDIA Corporation (\texttt{NVDA}) within the GMV portfolio. Despite NVIDIA exhibiting a high standalone volatility of 52.86 percent, its pairwise correlation with domestic Philippine equities is exceptionally low, registering at 0.1895 with \texttt{TEL} and 0.0121 with \texttt{MER}. In matrix terms, the marginal risk derivative $\partial \sigma_p / \partial w_i = (\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{w})_i / \sigma_p$ demonstrates that introducing an uncorrelated foreign asset dampens aggregate portfolio variance because the off-diagonal covariance savings outweigh the small diagonal variance contribution. Conversely, Meta Platforms (\texttt{META}) and Bitcoin (\texttt{BTC}) receive zero allocation. Bitcoin's extreme annualized volatility of 62.38 percent imposes an excessive diagonal variance penalty that cannot be counterbalanced by diversification, causing its dual non-negativity multiplier $\nu_{\text{BTC}}^*$ to bind strictly positive. The resulting GMV portfolio delivers an annualized expected return of 9.42 percent with an annualized volatility of 14.28 percent, achieving a Sharpe ratio of 0.2917.

In contrast, the Maximum Sharpe Ratio portfolio shifts capital aggressively into high-momentum technology growth engines. The tangency vector allocates 58.36 percent to NVIDIA Corporation (\texttt{NVDA}), 22.60 percent to Meta Platforms, Inc. (\texttt{META}), and 19.04 percent to Manila Electric Company (\texttt{MER}). The heavy concentration in \texttt{NVDA} and \texttt{META} reflects the compounding capital appreciation experienced by US large-capitalization technology firms during the sample period, which expands the expected excess return numerator $(\mathbf{w}^T \boldsymbol{\mu} - R_f)$ at a rate that far outpaces the expansion of portfolio variance in the denominator.

Manila Electric Company (\texttt{MER}) serves as the sole domestic anchor within the Maximum Sharpe portfolio, capturing nearly a fifth of total allocation. \texttt{MER} combines a strong historical return trajectory with utility dividend stability and near-zero correlation with global technology equities ($r_{\text{MER, NVDA}} = 0.0121$, $r_{\text{MER, META}} = 0.0172$). By incorporating \texttt{MER}, the optimizer mitigates currency and equity volatility shocks without diluting overall return generation. Telecommunications (\texttt{TEL}) and industrial conglomerates (\texttt{AEV}) are excluded from the tangency portfolio because their lower expected returns dilute the Sharpe numerator. Bitcoin (\texttt{BTC}) is also assigned a zero weight because its 62.38 percent annualized volatility expands the risk denominator disproportionately relative to its incremental return contribution. The resulting Maximum Sharpe portfolio achieves an annualized expected return of 49.82 percent and an annualized volatility of 32.14 percent, generating an industry-leading Sharpe ratio of 1.3868.

\subsection{Geometry of the Efficient Frontier and Capital Allocation Line}

Figure~\ref{fig:efficient_frontier} illustrates the Markowitz Efficient Frontier generated across 50 target return increments, beginning at the GMV vertex and terminating at the maximum return boundary, overlaid with the Capital Allocation Line.

The geometric properties displayed in Figure~\ref{fig:efficient_frontier} illustrate the core principles of portfolio efficiency. The efficient frontier hyperbola originates at the GMV coordinate $(\sigma = 14.28\%, \mu = 9.42\%)$ and curves upward with diminishing marginal returns to incremental volatility. Individual asset coordinates, notably \texttt{BTC} ($\sigma = 62.38\%$) and \texttt{META} ($\sigma = 44.29\%$), lie far to the right of the efficient boundary. The horizontal distance separating these standalone assets from the frontier quantifies the structural diversification gain, proving that holding unhedged standalone positions violates mean-variance efficiency.

The Capital Allocation Line originates at the risk-free rate intercept $(0, R_f = 5.25\%)$ and forms a single tangency point with the risky asset envelope precisely at the Tangency Portfolio $(\sigma = 32.14\%, \mu = 49.82\%)$. The slope of this ray equals the maximum Sharpe ratio ($S_p = 1.3868$). Under the separation theorem, any institutional investor can construct an optimal portfolio along this linear trajectory by combining the Tangency portfolio with risk-free lending or borrowing, establishing complete dominance over any individual risky asset allocation.
