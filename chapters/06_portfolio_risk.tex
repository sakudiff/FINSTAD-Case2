\section{Portfolio Risk Assessment}

While traditional mean-variance framework relies on second-moment variance as the sole measure of risk, empirical asset returns in financial markets consistently violate Gaussian normality. Daily asset returns exhibit pronounced negative skewness, volatility clustering, and heavy tails (leptokurtosis). Consequently, relying solely on standard deviation significantly underestimates the probability and severity of catastrophic drawdowns.

To provide an institutional-grade risk assessment, this section evaluates downside vulnerability and tail risk across the Global Minimum Variance (GMV) and Maximum Sharpe Ratio (Tangency) portfolios using Value at Risk (VaR), Expected Shortfall (CVaR), multivariate normal Monte Carlo simulations, and historical crisis stress scenario testing.

\subsection{Mathematical and Econometric Foundations of Downside Risk Measures}

\subsubsection{Value at Risk (VaR) Formulation}

Let $L = -\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{r}$ denote the loss random variable of a portfolio with weight vector $\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and daily return vector $\mathbf{r} \in \mathbb{R}^n$. The Value at Risk at confidence level $\alpha \in (0, 1)$ over a 1-day holding horizon is defined as the $\alpha$-quantile of the portfolio loss distribution:

\begin{equation}
  \text{VaR}_\alpha(\mathbf{w}) = \inf \left\{ l \in \mathbb{R} : P(L > l) \le 1 - \alpha \right\} = -F_R^{-1}(1 - \alpha)
\end{equation}

where $F_R(r)$ is the cumulative distribution function of portfolio return $R = \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{r}$. We implement two complementary estimations:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Non-Parametric Historical VaR}: Calculated directly from the empirical quantile of the historical return series without distributional assumptions:
  \begin{equation}
    \text{VaR}_\alpha^{\text{Hist}}(\mathbf{w}) = -\widehat{F}_n^{-1}(1 - \alpha) = -R_{(\lfloor N(1-\alpha) \rfloor)}
  \end{equation}
  where $R_{(k)}$ denotes the $k$-th order statistic of the $N = 1,509$ daily return observations.

  \item \textbf{Parametric Gaussian VaR}: Assumes that returns follow a multivariate normal distribution $\mathbf{r} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{daily}}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{daily}})$:
  \begin{equation}
    \text{VaR}_\alpha^{\text{Gauss}}(\mathbf{w}) = -\left( \mathbf{w}^T \boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{daily}} + z_{1-\alpha} \sqrt{\mathbf{w}^T \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{daily}} \mathbf{w}} \right)
  \end{equation}
  where $z_{1-\alpha} = \Phi^{-1}(1 - \alpha)$ is the standard normal inverse cumulative distribution value ($z_{0.05} \approx -1.6449$ for $\alpha = 0.95$, and $z_{0.01} \approx -2.3263$ for $\alpha = 0.99$).
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Expected Shortfall (CVaR) and Coherent Risk Axioms}

While VaR is widely adopted in regulatory frameworks, it exhibits critical theoretical limitations: it provides no information regarding the severity of losses exceeding the quantile threshold, and it is non-subadditive under fat-tailed or asymmetric distributions (i.e., $\text{VaR}(X + Y) > \text{VaR}(X) + \text{VaR}(Y)$ is possible, discouraging portfolio diversification).

To overcome these limitations, Expected Shortfall ($\text{ES}_\alpha$, also termed Conditional Value at Risk or CVaR) measures the conditional expectation of loss given that the loss strictly exceeds the $\text{VaR}_\alpha$ threshold:

\begin{equation}
  \text{ES}_\alpha(\mathbf{w}) = \mathbb{E} \left[ L \mid L \ge \text{VaR}_\alpha(\mathbf{w}) \right] = \frac{1}{1 - \alpha} \int_{\alpha}^{1} \text{VaR}_u(\mathbf{w}) \, du
\end{equation}

As established by \textcite{artzner1999}, Expected Shortfall is a strictly \textit{coherent risk measure} that satisfies four fundamental axioms:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Translation Invariance}: $\text{ES}_\alpha(L + c) = \text{ES}_\alpha(L) + c$ for any constant cash amount $c$.
  \item \textbf{Subadditivity}: $\text{ES}_\alpha(L_1 + L_2) \le \text{ES}_\alpha(L_1) + \text{ES}_\alpha(L_2)$, ensuring that merging portfolio risks never increases aggregate risk.
  \item \textbf{Positive Homogeneity}: $\text{ES}_\alpha(\lambda L) = \lambda \text{ES}_\alpha(L)$ for all $\lambda > 0$.
  \item \textbf{Monotonicity}: If $L_1 \le L_2$ almost surely, then $\text{ES}_\alpha(L_1) \le \text{ES}_\alpha(L_2)$.
\end{enumerate}

Furthermore, following \textcite{fabozzi2002}, Expected Shortfall can be calculated parametrically under normality as:

\begin{equation}
  \text{ES}_\alpha^{\text{Gauss}}(\mathbf{w}) = -\mathbf{w}^T \boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{daily}} + \sigma_p \frac{\phi(z_{1-\alpha})}{1 - \alpha}
\end{equation}

where $\phi(\cdot)$ is the standard normal probability density function and $\sigma_p = \sqrt{\mathbf{w}^T \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{daily}} \mathbf{w}}$.

    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Multivariate Monte Carlo Return Simulation and Distributional Diagnostics}

To assess potential portfolio return distributions beyond the finite historical sample, a 10,000-draw Monte Carlo simulation was executed using the multivariate normal covariance structure. The simulation generates synthetic return vectors $\mathbf{r}_{\text{sim}} \in \mathbb{R}^6$ using the Cholesky factorization $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{daily}} = \mathbf{L} \mathbf{L}^T$:

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{r}_{\text{sim}} = \boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{daily}} + \mathbf{L} \mathbf{z}, \quad \mathbf{z} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I}_6)
\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}

  \item \textbf{Fat-Tail Divergence}: The empirical left tail extends substantially further into extreme negative territory than the Gaussian curve. Gaussian models assign negligible probability ($\approx 0.003\%$) to 4-sigma daily losses, whereas empirical financial time series experience such drawdowns with much higher frequency due to systemic liquidity shocks and earnings surprises.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Crisis Stress Testing and Macroeconomic Scenario Analysis}

To test structural solvency and capital preservation during systemic market turmoil, five historical and hypothetical crisis scenarios were simulated:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{2020 COVID-19 Liquidity Shock (Feb 19 -- Mar 23, 2020)}: Global financial market selloff characterized by margin calls, synchronized cross-asset liquidations, and flight to cash.
  \item \textbf{2022 Crypto Winter and Stablecoin Contagion (May 5 -- May 13, 2022)}: Collapse of algorithmic stablecoins (Terra/Luna) and institutional DeFi deleveraging.
  \item \textbf{2022 Federal Reserve Aggressive Rate Hike Shock (Jun 8 -- Jun 16, 2022)}: Rapid 75-basis-point policy rate tightening cycle and global discount rate repricing.
  \item \textbf{Tech Sector Multiple De-rating (Nov 19, 2021 -- Jan 27, 2022)}: Post-stimulus valuation compression in long-duration growth and semiconductor equities.
  \item \textbf{Hypothetical 1.5x Black Swan Event}: A composite stress shock applying a 1.5 times multiplier to the maximum observed historical drawdown across all assets simultaneously.
\end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{COVID-19 Shock Resilience}: During the 2020 pandemic crash, the GMV portfolio limited capital drawdown to -13.82 percent, whereas the Maximum Sharpe portfolio suffered a severe -29.84 percent contraction (2.16 times greater capital loss). Domestic utilities and telecoms maintained operational cash flows, dampening the portfolio drawdown.
  \item \textbf{Insulation from Crypto Contagion}: During the 2022 Crypto Winter, the GMV portfolio experienced a minor -1.45 percent pullback due to complete absence of digital assets, while the Maximum Sharpe portfolio declined -12.18 percent due to co-movement with general risk-off sentiment.
  \item \textbf{Interest Rate Duration Sensitivity}: During the 2022 Fed rate tightening, the GMV portfolio fell only -3.12 percent compared to -14.65 percent for the Maximum Sharpe portfolio. Growth technology equities possess high equity duration, making their discounted present values highly sensitive to rising interest rates ($r_{\text{discount}} \uparrow \implies \text{PV} \downarrow$).
  \item \textbf{Tech Sector Valuation Shock}: The tech selloff caused a -22.40 percent drawdown in the Maximum Sharpe portfolio due to its 80.96 percent tech allocation, while the GMV portfolio lost only -4.85 percent.
  \item \textbf{Black Swan Solvency Shock}: Under the hypothetical 1.5x Black Swan shock, the GMV portfolio drawdown was contained at -19.45 percent, whereas the Maximum Sharpe portfolio suffered a catastrophic -44.76 percent capital loss.
\end{itemize}

These findings demonstrate the fundamental trade-off of quantitative portfolio management: while the Maximum Sharpe allocation achieves superior wealth compounding during secular growth regimes, the GMV allocation provides indispensable capital preservation during systemic tail crises.
