\section{Portfolio Risk Assessment}

While traditional mean-variance optimization relies on return variance as a symmetric measure of risk, empirical financial time series consistently violate Gaussian normality. Daily asset returns exhibit pronounced negative skewness, volatility clustering, and heavy tails (leptokurtosis). Consequently, relying solely on standard deviation significantly underestimates the probability and severity of catastrophic tail losses during periods of market turmoil.

To establish an institutional-grade risk evaluation, this section assesses downside vulnerability across the Global Minimum Variance (GMV) and Maximum Sharpe Ratio (Tangency) portfolios using non-parametric Value at Risk (VaR), coherent Expected Shortfall (CVaR), multivariate normal Monte Carlo simulations, and historical crisis stress testing.

\subsection{Econometric Foundations of Downside Risk and Coherent Risk Measures}

\subsubsection{Value at Risk Formulation}

Let $L = -\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{r}$ denote the daily loss random variable of a portfolio with weight vector $\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and daily log return vector $\mathbf{r} \in \mathbb{R}^n$. The Value at Risk at confidence level $\alpha \in (0, 1)$ over a 1-day holding horizon is defined as the $\alpha$-quantile of the portfolio loss distribution:

\begin{equation}
  \text{VaR}_\alpha(\mathbf{w}) = \inf \left\{ l \in \mathbb{R} : P(L > l) \le 1 - \alpha \right\} = -F_R^{-1}(1 - \alpha)
\end{equation}

where $F_R(r)$ is the cumulative distribution function of portfolio return $R = \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{r}$. We compute two distinct specifications:

Non-parametric Historical VaR is calculated directly from the empirical quantile of observed sample returns without imposing distributional assumptions:

\begin{equation}
  \text{VaR}_\alpha^{\text{Hist}}(\mathbf{w}) = -\widehat{F}_n^{-1}(1 - \alpha) = -R_{(\lfloor N(1-\alpha) \rfloor)}
\end{equation}

where $R_{(k)}$ denotes the $k$-th order statistic across the $N = 1,509$ daily trading observations.

Parametric Gaussian VaR assumes that portfolio returns follow a stationary normal distribution $\mathbf{r} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{daily}}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{daily}})$:

\begin{equation}
  \text{VaR}_\alpha^{\text{Gauss}}(\mathbf{w}) = -\left( \mathbf{w}^T \boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{daily}} + z_{1-\alpha} \sqrt{\mathbf{w}^T \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{daily}} \mathbf{w}} \right)
\end{equation}

where $z_{1-\alpha} = \Phi^{-1}(1 - \alpha)$ is the standard normal inverse cumulative distribution value ($z_{0.05} \approx -1.6449$ for $\alpha = 0.95$, and $z_{0.01} \approx -2.3263$ for $\alpha = 0.99$).

\subsubsection{Expected Shortfall and Coherent Risk Axioms}

Although Value at Risk remains the standard benchmark in financial regulation, it suffers from two major theoretical flaws: it is completely silent regarding the magnitude of losses exceeding the quantile threshold, and it fails the mathematical property of subadditivity under non-normal return distributions. When subadditivity is violated, combining portfolios can produce an aggregate VaR greater than the sum of individual VaRs, penalizing diversification.

To address these limitations, Expected Shortfall ($\text{ES}_\alpha$, or Conditional Value at Risk) measures the conditional expectation of loss given that the loss strictly exceeds the $\text{VaR}_\alpha$ threshold:

\begin{equation}
  \text{ES}_\alpha(\mathbf{w}) = \mathbb{E} \left[ L \mid L \ge \text{VaR}_\alpha(\mathbf{w}) \right] = \frac{1}{1 - \alpha} \int_{\alpha}^{1} \text{VaR}_u(\mathbf{w}) \, du
\end{equation}

As proven by \textcite{artzner1999}, Expected Shortfall constitutes a strictly coherent risk measure by satisfying four fundamental axioms: Translation Invariance, Subadditivity ($\text{ES}_\alpha(L_1 + L_2) \le \text{ES}_\alpha(L_1) + \text{ES}_\alpha(L_2)$), Positive Homogeneity, and Monotonicity. Under Gaussian normality, Expected Shortfall is computed analytically following \textcite{fabozzi2002}:

\begin{equation}
  \text{ES}_\alpha^{\text{Gauss}}(\mathbf{w}) = -\mathbf{w}^T \boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{daily}} + \sigma_p \frac{\phi(z_{1-\alpha})}{1 - \alpha}
\end{equation}

where $\phi(\cdot)$ is the standard normal probability density function and $\sigma_p = \sqrt{\mathbf{w}^T \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{daily}} \mathbf{w}}$.

\input{tables/tbl_var_summary.tex}

The empirical metrics in Table~\ref{tab:var_summary} reveal substantial divergence in downside vulnerability between the two portfolio allocations.

The Global Minimum Variance portfolio demonstrates exceptional downside containment. Over a daily horizon, its 95 percent Historical VaR is limited to 1.32 percent, with an Expected Shortfall of 2.14 percent. Under extreme 99 percent tail conditions, the GMV portfolio experiences a Historical VaR of 2.45 percent and an Expected Shortfall of 3.25 percent per day. Comparing the Historical metrics against Parametric Gaussian estimates ($\text{VaR}_{95} = 1.48\%$, $\text{ES}_{95} = 1.86\%$) confirms that the domestic utility and telecom allocation (\texttt{TEL}, \texttt{AEV}, \texttt{MER}) exhibits stable, near-Gaussian return behavior with minimal tail risk amplification.

Conversely, the Maximum Sharpe Ratio portfolio exhibits severe tail vulnerability driven by its 80.96 percent aggregate concentration in US growth equities (\texttt{NVDA}: 58.36 percent, \texttt{META}: 22.60 percent). Its daily 95 percent Historical VaR reaches 2.61 percent (nearly double that of the GMV portfolio), while its 95 percent Expected Shortfall expands to 4.12 percent. At the 99 percent confidence level, the Maximum Sharpe portfolio incurs a Historical VaR of 4.88 percent and an empirical Expected Shortfall of 6.25 percent per day.

Crucially, the empirical 99 percent Expected Shortfall (6.25 percent) exceeds the Gaussian parametric prediction (5.38 percent) by 87 basis points per day, representing a 16.2 percent relative underestimation error. This discrepancy demonstrates the presence of pronounced negative tail jumps in technology equities, proving that conventional Gaussian risk models dangerously understate actual capital requirements during severe market corrections.

\subsection{Multivariate Monte Carlo Return Simulation and Density Geometry}

To evaluate potential return trajectories beyond the observed historical sample, a 10,000-draw Monte Carlo simulation was executed using the multivariate normal covariance structure of the six assets. Synthetic return vectors $\mathbf{r}_{\text{sim}} \in \mathbb{R}^6$ were generated via the Cholesky factorization $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{daily}} = \mathbf{L} \mathbf{L}^T$, where $\mathbf{r}_{\text{sim}} = \boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{daily}} + \mathbf{L} \mathbf{z}$ and $\mathbf{z} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I}_6)$.

A granular economic analysis of Table~\ref{tab:stress_summary} and Figure~\ref{fig:stress_test} demonstrates the capital preservation properties of the GMV allocation during market dislocations. During the 2020 COVID-19 pandemic crash, the GMV portfolio limited capital loss to -13.82 percent, whereas the Maximum Sharpe portfolio suffered a steep -29.84 percent drawdown (2.16 times greater capital loss) due to synchronized global equity selling. During the 2022 Crypto Winter, the GMV portfolio declined by only -1.45 percent due to complete exclusion of digital assets, while the Maximum Sharpe portfolio lost -12.18 percent.

The 2022 Federal Reserve rate tightening shock further highlights the equity duration risk embedded in high-growth technology equities. Because growth equities derive a substantial portion of their valuation from distant future cash flows, rising discount rates compressed their present values, inflicting a -14.65 percent drawdown on the Maximum Sharpe portfolio compared to -3.12 percent for GMV. Similarly, during the broader tech valuation compression, the Maximum Sharpe portfolio fell -22.40 percent, while the GMV portfolio lost only -4.85 percent. Under the hypothetical 1.5x Black Swan shock, the GMV drawdown was contained at -19.45 percent, whereas the Maximum Sharpe portfolio suffered a catastrophic -44.76 percent capital loss.

These findings highlight the core trade-off governing quantitative portfolio construction. While the Maximum Sharpe allocation delivers superior wealth compounding during sustained economic expansions, the Global Minimum Variance allocation provides indispensable structural protection against systemic financial crises.
